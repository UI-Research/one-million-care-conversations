What I would change about my mother's access to care

I am my mother's only daughter (she has two sons) and her power of attorney for health. She has dementia that was diagnosed approximately 8 years ago. She has Kaiser Permanente health insurance through her widow's (my father's) employment, and Medicare. The only resources I have been made aware of for her, and for me as her only family care giver, are through hospice. That means that - unless something changes otherwise - until her death is imminent:

- She actually has no regular direct contact with a care provider; all her non-emergency medical needs are mediated through me or a med tech to a family doctor's office that has not seen or spoken with her in years. (For all the Kaiser patients who don't have a family member actively engaged in this role, that means, as we know, their only contact with Kaiser is through the facility in which they reside, if they're in one.)

- There is also no access to meeting emergent care needs in place. This requires an extraordinarily costly emergency room visit for purposes of legal liability on the part of the facility where she stays, but at which nothing can be done for her. Quite the contrary, rather than prioritizing her comfort, the trips back and forth and the visit itself are very difficult and impact her health negatively. The actors who benefit from this may be the vendors, insurance companies, etc., but they are not the patient or family members. On top of the stress/blood pressure spike, being cold and hungry, lack of sleep, impact on family members, and the medical costs, there are also the $700 of incurred transportation fees: $350 each way in an ambulance for the 4.3 mile ride.

- Likewise, there is no access to support in terms of daily life and her general well being; I am her only support outside of what her facility provides. There is no social worker checking on us. There is no volunteer visiting my mother. There are no physical or occupational therapists or home health check ups, no pets or children or music or art or any kind of mental heath support.

My mother's late-stage dementia is a debilitating, terminal disease. Because of the way the insurance/Medicare/"health care" plan is designed, she has access to neither on-site resources (like her own assigned PCP, or a support group or therapist) or to the resources made available through hospice.

What my experience suggests is that there is an entire realm of resources missing for patients living with the debilitating, terminal diagnosis of Alzheimer's and other forms of dementia for whom death is not imminent.

Currently, those resources are only available through hospice, for which she does not qualify. This leaves us with limited, inappropriate forms of access to medical care, and virtually no support, mental health or social services. These services she (and I) are being denied are the same services made available to patients who are 1) able to access them independently, or 2) on hospice, in which case they are brought to her. For those of us left in between, we are left without these forms of care, and left to our own devices to provide them as we can on our own. As the disease progresses, and the person with that

disease becomes increasingly dependent, the sense of isolation and lack of support increases, making this time of life sad and despairing when it could instead contain more experiences of contented engagement and connection. These are the goals for care for someone with these illnesses as I understand them from the training I received through the Alzheimer's Association.

The solution to this problem does not require finding "more" resources; it seems like it would rather require making accessible the same resources due her that others receive, and based on actual need. Concretely, here are a few ideas for creating access that would make an enormous difference:

An "interim" diagnosis of mid-to-late-stage Alzheimer's or other form of dementia that determines eligibility

A regular nurses visit (quarterly, monthly, bi-monthly or so) to identify emergent needs, preventative care, etc.

An ad hoc nurse visit in place of a required emergency room visit (such a better use of resources for everyone)

An assigned social worker that checks on folks monthly/quarterly, just as they do weekly on hospice.

A volunteer pool like the one coordinated by the hospice department, making weekly or bi-monthly visits to spend time with people who are otherwise incredibly isolated.

A roaming pool of support animals that visit different facilities monthly, as a service made available to facilities that house patients served by Kaiser (or whoever).

A program bringing people with these "interim" diagnosis (and their broader community) into community with children, students, students of health care, health care interns, to the benefit of everyone involved etc. This would require not much more than a full-time program coordinator making connections between residential facilities, potential visiting communities, and the already-trained volunteer pool.

An "engagement advocate" who's task is to support the provision of meaningful, personalized care by making an initial visit and perhaps quarterly follow ups at which they build and maintain alignment between:

1) the person's actual (intrinsic and evolving) interests, needs and abilities 2) their care plan

3) additional available activities

4) additional resources available to the[m] through their insurance/Medicare 5) requests or unmet needs they can identify and report
